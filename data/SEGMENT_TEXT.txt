The weather station on Corwin Ridge had been running for almost forty years before anyone thought to replace the instruments. Every morning at six, the caretaker climbed the narrow path behind the visitor center, unlocked the white wooden enclosure, and wrote down the temperature, the humidity, and the overnight rainfall in a notebook with a cracked green cover. She didn't trust the digital sensors that the regional office had installed the previous spring. They were accurate enough, she admitted, but they never noticed anything interesting. A thermometer could tell you that the air was cold; it couldn't tell you that the frost had drawn feathered patterns across the railing, or that a fox had walked through the fresh snow sometime before dawn.

Her name was Margaret Okafor, and she had grown up in the valley below. Her father had repaired tractors and irrigation pumps, and her mother had taught chemistry at the consolidated high school, where the laboratory windows faced the mountains. Margaret remembered sitting on the counter after class, watching her mother calibrate a balance with tiny brass weights while explaining that measurement was mostly a matter of patience. You could buy a better instrument, her mother said, but you couldn't buy the habit of checking it twice.

That habit turned out to be useful. In the autumn of her third year at the station, Margaret noticed that the rain gauge was reporting slightly less water than the old copper cylinder she kept as a backup. The difference was small, only a few millimeters each week, but it was consistent. She photographed both gauges, compared the readings in a spreadsheet, and sent a polite message to the regional office. Nobody replied for a month. When a technician finally arrived, he discovered that a family of wrens had built a nest inside the funnel of the automatic gauge, diverting part of every storm into a tidy cup of twigs and moss. He removed the nest carefully, after the birds had left, and recalibrated the sensor while Margaret poured coffee from a dented thermos.

Down in the valley, the town of Westbrook was changing in ways that the weather records only hinted at. The cannery had closed, and the old brick building now housed a bakery, a bicycle workshop, and a small recording studio that rented rooms by the hour. On Saturday mornings, the parking lot filled with folding tables where farmers sold honey, cheese, mushrooms, and enormous bundles of rhubarb. A retired librarian named Theodore organized a seed exchange beside the fountain, labeling every envelope in meticulous handwriting: purple bush beans, cherry tomatoes, sunflowers that would grow taller than a delivery truck. Children wandered between the stalls with paper cups of lemonade, and the bakery's cinnamon bread usually sold out before nine.

The bicycle workshop belonged to two brothers, Rafael and Tomás, who had moved north from the coast after their uncle left them his tools. Neither of them had planned to repair bicycles for a living. Rafael had studied architecture and still sketched buildings on the backs of receipts, while Tomás had worked as a translator for a shipping company and could argue about grammar in four languages. Their workshop smelled of rubber, grease, and the oranges they ate for lunch. Customers often stayed long after their repairs were finished, drinking tea and debating whether the town should build a protected cycling lane along the river.

That debate became surprisingly heated. At a crowded council meeting in February, residents lined up to speak for exactly three minutes each. A dentist worried about parking. A teenager presented a map showing how many students rode to school along the busiest road. An elderly beekeeper described the afternoon a delivery van had clipped his handlebar and scattered a crate of jars across the pavement. Rafael brought architectural drawings of a narrow, tree-lined path separated from traffic by planters, and Tomás translated the comments of several newcomers who were still learning English. The council postponed its decision, as councils usually do, but agreed to commission a study.

The study required data, and data required patience. Volunteers stood at intersections with clipboards, counting pedestrians, cyclists, cars, and the occasional horse. Margaret contributed decades of rainfall and temperature records, which showed that the river path flooded far less often than people assumed. Theodore searched the library archives and found photographs from a century earlier, when the same route had been a towpath for barges carrying lumber downstream. The photographs were blurry and scratched, but they made the proposal feel less like an invention and more like a restoration.

By late summer, the consultants delivered their report. It was longer than anyone wanted and filled with charts that few people read closely, but its conclusion was clear: a protected path along the river would be safer, cheaper to maintain than widening the road, and unlikely to flood more than twice a year. The council approved the first section in September. Construction crews arrived in October, and for several weeks the riverbank echoed with the sound of excavators, generators, and workers calling to one another over the noise.

Margaret walked the finished section on the first frosty morning in November. The planters were still bare, waiting for spring, and the asphalt was so new that it shone like wet slate. A woman pushed a stroller past her, followed by a boy wobbling on a bicycle with training wheels. Near the old cannery, she spotted Rafael adjusting the brakes on a commuter bike while Tomás held a steaming mug and pointed at something across the water. Margaret raised a hand, and they waved back.

When she returned to the station that afternoon, she opened the cracked green notebook and recorded the usual measurements: temperature, humidity, rainfall, wind. Then, beneath the numbers, she added a short line that no sensor could have detected. River path open. Frost on the planters. Wrens nesting again in the hawthorn by the fence. She closed the notebook, locked the enclosure, and walked back down the hill as the light faded behind the ridge.
